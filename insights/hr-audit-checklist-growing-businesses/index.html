HR Audit Checklist for Growing Businesses: Review One HR Area at a Time

A full HR audit can feel like too big a project to start. It does not need to be done all at once. This checklist breaks HR into seven areas, so management can review one area properly — with the right questions, evidence and red flags — before moving to the next.

By PathWeavePublished 29 September 2026

In short

  • Review HR one area at a time. A focused review of one area produces more action than a superficial review of everything.
  • For each area, compare three things: what is written down, what actually happens, and what the evidence shows.
  • Classify each finding as in place, inconsistent or missing, then fix the few gaps that matter most.
  • This is a management review tool, not a legal or statutory compliance audit.

Scope of this checklist. This is an operational and management review guide to help leaders find gaps in HR practice. It is not legal advice and it is not a statutory compliance audit. If the review raises a question about legal or regulatory obligations, take advice from a qualified professional — particularly as India's labour law framework was consolidated into four Labour Codes from 21 November 2025.

How to use this checklist

An HR audit — or HR gap analysis — is most useful when it answers a practical question: what in this area is working, what is inconsistent, and what is missing? The mistake most businesses make is to try to review all of HR at once. The review becomes a long list of observations with no clear priority, and very little changes.

Instead, pick the area causing the most friction or risk. Then:

  1. Collect the written position — policies, process notes, templates, system configuration.
  2. Check actual practice — talk to a few managers and employees, and look at recent real cases.
  3. Test the evidence — sample records to see whether what should exist does exist.
  4. Classify the findings as in place, inconsistent or missing.
  5. Prioritise the two or three gaps with the greatest cost, risk or employee impact.

The seven areas below follow the same structure: what to inspect, questions to ask, evidence to review, red flags and a common gap pattern.

1. HR Operations

What to inspect: the recurring HR work that keeps the organisation running — letters, confirmations, leave administration, attendance, payroll inputs, employee requests — and who owns each piece.

Questions to ask

  • Is there a list of recurring HR activities with a named owner for each?
  • Are there agreed timelines — for example, for issuing letters or processing requests?
  • Can anyone see what is pending and overdue?
  • What happens when the person who usually does the work is absent?

Evidence to review: a sample of recent employee requests and how long they took; the payroll input file for a recent month; any HR tracker or ticket log.

  • Work is tracked in one person's inbox or memory.
  • Employees chase HR repeatedly for routine documents.
  • Payroll inputs are regularly late or corrected after processing.

Common gap pattern: HR operations run adequately while one experienced person is present, but nothing is documented, so quality drops sharply during absence or after that person leaves.

2. Policies & Compliance

What to inspect: the policies the business has, whether they are current and known, whether managers apply them consistently, and whether the business knows which employment and payroll obligations apply to it.

Questions to ask

  • Is there a current employee handbook, and when was it last reviewed?
  • Do employees acknowledge key policies, and can they find them?
  • Do managers apply leave, attendance and conduct rules the same way?
  • Does the business maintain a list of the statutory obligations that apply to it, with owners and due dates?
  • Has that list been reviewed by a qualified professional since the Labour Codes took effect?

Evidence to review: the handbook and policy documents; policy acknowledgements; a sample of recent leave or disciplinary cases; the business's compliance calendar or tracker.

  • Policies were copied from another company and never adapted.
  • Exceptions are routinely agreed verbally and not recorded.
  • Nobody can say who owns the statutory compliance calendar.

Common gap pattern: written policies exist, but practice has drifted away from them — so the documents describe a business that no longer exists.

3. Performance Management

What to inspect: how expectations are set, how often performance is discussed, how reviews are conducted and how outcomes link to development and reward.

Questions to ask

  • Does each employee have written goals or expectations for the current period?
  • Do managers hold regular check-ins, or only an annual review?
  • Were the last review cycle's reviews completed on time across all teams?
  • How are ratings or assessments calibrated across managers?
  • How are underperformance cases handled and documented?

Evidence to review: goal documents for a sample of employees; completion records from the last cycle; a sample of completed reviews; any performance improvement plans.

  • Review completion varies widely between teams.
  • Most employees receive the same rating.
  • Increments are decided before reviews are completed.
  • Underperformance is raised for the first time at the annual review.

Common gap pattern: the performance process exists as a form and a calendar, but not as a management practice — goals are vague and conversations are irregular.

4. Recruitment

What to inspect: how roles are approved and defined, how candidates are assessed, how offers are made and how new hires are onboarded.

Questions to ask

  • Is each opening approved against a budget or headcount plan?
  • Does each role have a written description with outcomes before hiring starts?
  • Do interviewers assess against agreed criteria and record their assessment?
  • Are offers made using standard formats, with exceptions approved?
  • Does each new joiner have a planned onboarding?

Evidence to review: the last several hires' role descriptions, interview records, offer letters and onboarding plans; time-to-hire and early-attrition data if available.

  • Offer terms for similar roles differ without explanation.
  • Interview feedback is informal or not recorded.
  • New joiners leave within their first months with no review of why.

Common gap pattern: each hiring manager runs their own process, so hiring quality and candidate experience depend on who is hiring.

5. Organisation & Role Clarity

What to inspect: the organisation structure, reporting lines, role definitions, spans of control and who owns which decisions.

Questions to ask

  • Is there a current organisation chart that matches reality?
  • Does every role have a current description of purpose and responsibilities?
  • Are there roles where two people believe they own the same thing — or where nobody does?
  • Are any managers managing too many people to manage them properly?
  • Is it clear which decisions managers can take without escalation?

Evidence to review: the organisation chart; role descriptions; a list of recent decisions that were escalated to leadership; recent cross-team disputes.

  • Everything is escalated to the founder or CEO.
  • Titles do not match responsibilities.
  • Employees cannot say clearly who their manager is for different parts of their work.

Common gap pattern: the organisation grew by adding people to solve immediate problems, so the structure reflects history rather than how the business needs to operate now.

6. HRMS & HR Processes

What to inspect: the systems and workflows supporting HR — HRMS, spreadsheets, payroll tools, document storage — and whether they reduce work or create it.

Questions to ask

  • Which system is the single source of truth for employee data?
  • Is employee data complete and accurate in that system?
  • Which HR processes run through system workflows, and which run on email?
  • Can leadership get headcount, joiner, leaver and attrition figures without manual work?
  • Who has access to which employee data, and is access reviewed?

Evidence to review: a sample of employee records in the system against source documents; standard reports; the access list; the list of features in use versus paid for.

  • The HRMS and spreadsheets hold different versions of the same data.
  • Monthly reports require manual reconciliation.
  • Former employees or staff still have system access.

Common gap pattern: an HRMS was implemented, but only partly configured and partly adopted, so the business pays for a system while still running HR on spreadsheets.

7. Employee Lifecycle

What to inspect: the key employee events — joining, probation, confirmation, role changes, transfers and exits — and whether each is handled consistently and on time.

Questions to ask

  • Is there a checklist and owner for each lifecycle event?
  • Are confirmations completed on time after probation?
  • Are role and salary changes documented and communicated in writing?
  • Are exits handled through a defined clearance process with timely final settlement?
  • Are exit reasons captured and reviewed?

Evidence to review: the files of recent joiners and leavers; confirmation dates against probation end dates; full-and-final settlement timelines; exit interview records.

  • Employees work past probation without a confirmation decision.
  • Promotions or transfers happen without written communication.
  • Final settlements are routinely delayed.

Common gap pattern: joining gets attention because it is visible; confirmation, internal changes and exits get far less, and that is where employee trust and documentation both erode.

Summarising your findings

For the area you reviewed, summarise the findings in a simple table: each item, its status (in place, inconsistent or missing), the evidence, the impact and a proposed owner. Then choose no more than three priorities. The goal is a short list of changes that will actually be made, not a comprehensive report.
