Fractional HR vs Outsourced HR vs HR Consulting: Which Fits an Indian SME?

HR consulting, Fractional HR, HR outsourcing and an internal HR team solve different problems. They are often compared as if they were interchangeable. This guide compares them side by side and gives a simple way to decide which fits the problem you actually have.

By PathWeavePublished 29 September 2026

In short

  • HR consulting solves a defined problem through a project with an end date.
  • Fractional HR provides ongoing senior HR leadership, part-time.
  • HR outsourcing runs recurring HR operations to agreed service levels.
  • Internal HR gives full-time presence and ownership inside the business.
  • Choose by the problem you need solved — design, direction or delivery — not by the label. Many SMEs end up with a combination.

Why the models get confused

From the outside, the three external HR models can look alike: an experienced HR person or firm, working with the business for a fee, on HR matters. Providers also blur the lines — consultants offer retainers, outsourcing firms offer advice, and fractional leaders sometimes end up doing administration.

The difference lies in what kind of problem each model is designed to solve. Broadly, an SME's HR need falls into one of three types:

  • Design — something needs to be created or fixed once: a policy framework, an organisation structure, a performance system.
  • Direction — the business needs ongoing senior judgement about people: priorities, difficult decisions, leadership advice.
  • Delivery — recurring HR work needs to happen reliably every month.

Each model maps most naturally to one of these.

HR consulting

HR consulting is project-based. The business engages a consultant to solve a defined problem — design a performance management system, restructure roles, create an employee handbook, review HR processes — with a scope, deliverables and an end point. Consulting suits design needs. Its limitation is continuity: once the project ends, someone inside the business has to own and sustain what was built.

Fractional HR

Fractional HR provides a senior HR leader for a defined share of their time, on an ongoing basis. It suits direction needs: setting the people agenda, advising leaders, guiding HR execution and handling decisions that need experienced judgement. It is not designed to provide high-volume administrative capacity. Our guide to when Fractional HR makes sense covers the model in depth.

HR outsourcing

HR outsourcing hands defined recurring processes — lifecycle administration, payroll processing, records, HRMS administration — to a provider under agreed service measures. It suits delivery needs. It does not replace management's responsibility for people decisions, and it does not by itself provide HR strategy. Our guide to what SMEs should outsource and retain goes further.

Internal HR

An internal HR team — from a single HR executive to a full function with a senior leader — gives the business daily presence, deep organisational knowledge and full-time ownership. It suits businesses where the volume and complexity of people work justifies permanent roles. Its constraints are cost and the difficulty of hiring senior HR capability before the business can fully use it.

Hybrid models

In practice, many growing SMEs combine models. Common combinations include:

  • Internal HR executive + Fractional HR leader — day-to-day operations in-house, senior direction from outside.
  • Outsourced operations + Fractional HR leader — recurring work run by a provider, with an experienced leader setting direction and holding the provider to standard.
  • Internal HR + project consulting — an established HR team bringing in specialists for a defined redesign.

Hybrids work well when the boundaries are explicit: who owns decisions, who runs processes and who reviews whom. They work badly when several parties each assume someone else is responsible.

A decision framework

Work through these questions in order:

  1. What is the problem? Describe it in business terms: hiring is inconsistent; joining formalities are late; managers avoid performance conversations; the founder spends two days a week on people issues.
  2. Is it design, direction or delivery? A one-off fix points to consulting. A continuing need for judgement points to Fractional HR or an internal leader. Recurring work that slips points to outsourcing or internal operations capacity.
  3. How much of it is there? If the volume would fill a full-time role, consider hiring. If not, an external model is usually more efficient.
  4. Who inside will own it? Every model needs an internal counterpart. If there is nobody, that is the first gap to close.
  5. How long will the need last? A temporary peak — a restructure, a hiring burst — favours external models that can scale down afterwards.

Cost drivers, qualitatively

We have deliberately not quoted prices, because they vary widely with scope. The drivers are predictable, though: consulting cost follows the size and complexity of the project; Fractional HR cost follows the seniority and amount of time committed; outsourcing cost follows the number of processes, employees and locations covered and the service levels required; internal HR cost follows salaries, management overhead and the systems the team needs. Compare models on the cost of solving the problem, not on hourly or monthly rates alone.

Questions founders should ask

  • What exactly will be different in six months if this works?
  • Who in our business will own this relationship and its outputs?
  • Which decisions stay with us, and which will the provider or leader make?
  • What happens when the engagement ends — who sustains the result?
  • How will we measure whether it is working?
  • If we need a different model later, how easily can we transition?

The common mistake: choosing the model before defining the problem

The most frequent error is starting with the model — "we need Fractional HR" or "let's outsource HR" — because someone recommended it or a peer company uses it. The result is a mismatch: a fractional leader buried in administration, an outsourcing provider asked to fix culture, or a consultant's framework that nobody implements.

Define the problem first, in business terms. Then decide whether it needs design, direction or delivery. The model follows.
